Why I'm Turning Dividends Into Crypto

By DivToCrypto · Oct 31, 2026 · 5 min read

This is a public experiment with my own money. The idea fits on an index card: buy monthly dividend stocks, let them pay me, and use those dividends to buy crypto. Every trade gets logged. Every result gets published, including the bad weeks. You can check the live numbers on the DivToCrypto dashboard any time.

This post explains why I'm doing it this way and the rules I've set for myself.

The problem I'm trying to solve

Crypto is the part of my portfolio I'm most excited about and the part I trust least. Prices move hard in both directions, and when I put fresh cash in, I tend to make decisions based on how I feel that day.

Dividend stocks are the opposite. They're boring on purpose. A solid monthly payer keeps sending cash whether crypto had a great week or a terrible one.

So instead of picking one or the other, I want the boring part to feed the exciting part. The dividend stocks are the engine. Crypto is where the engine's output goes.

How the dividends-to-crypto strategy works

  1. Build a base of monthly dividend payers. Stocks and ETFs that pay every month, not every quarter.
  2. Hold them. I'm a buy-and-hold person. The goal is to own good payers and let them keep paying.
  3. Route the dividends into crypto. When a dividend lands, it becomes a crypto buy. That's the whole point of the name.
  4. Log every trade. Date, asset, buy or sell, quantity, price, total, and a short note on why.
  5. Publish the P&L. The dashboard shows what I hold, every buy and sell, realized and unrealized profit or loss, and dividends received.

Right now everything runs through one Robinhood account: the crypto today, and the dividend stocks once I buy them.

Why monthly dividend stocks

Monthly payers fit this plan better than quarterly ones for a few practical reasons:

  • Smaller, more frequent crypto buys. Twelve smaller buys a year instead of four bigger ones. That spreads out my entry points and takes some of the timing guesswork away.
  • Faster feedback. With a small account, I want to see the machine working. A monthly payment, even a tiny one, shows up on the dashboard and keeps me honest.
  • Simpler tracking. One payment per holding per month is easy to log and easy to explain.

Monthly doesn't mean safer. A monthly payer can cut its dividend just like a quarterly one, and an unusually high yield is often a warning sign, not a gift. I go through the specific stocks and funds in The 20 Monthly Dividend Stocks I'm Building Around.

Why buy-and-hold instead of dividend capture

Dividend capture means buying right before the ex-dividend date and selling soon after to collect the payment. I've decided not to do that. A stock's price typically drops by about the dividend amount when it goes ex-dividend, so the "free money" usually isn't free. You also rack up more trades, more taxable events, and more chances to make a mistake.

I'd rather own payers I'm comfortable holding through a rough year and let the dividends feed the crypto side month after month.

The rules

These are the rules I'm holding myself to. Writing them down in public is part of how I plan to keep them.

Rule 1: Log everything

Every buy and every sell goes in the trade log, with a note when there's a reason worth remembering. No "I'll add it later." If it happened, it gets logged.

Rule 2: Publish everything

The dashboard is public, and losses stay on it. If I make a bad trade, you'll see it there and you'll probably read about it here. The only things that never go on the site are private details like account numbers and order IDs.

Rule 3: Dividends fund crypto buys

Dividend cash is earmarked for crypto. The dashboard tracks dividends received and how much has been reinvested into crypto, so anyone can check whether I'm actually following through.

Rule 4: No made-up numbers

Every figure I share comes from my trade log or the live dashboard. If I don't have a number, I'll say so instead of guessing.

Where things stand right now

I'll be straight about this: the dividend side doesn't exist yet. As of October 31, 2026, I don't own any dividend stocks and I haven't received a single dividend.

What I have done is start the crypto side with about $179 of my own money, and the first week didn't go well. I'm down roughly 7%, and about a third of that loss came from one short-term trade. I wrote the whole thing up in Week One: Down 7% and What ENA Taught Me.

So for now, "dividends into crypto" is a plan, not a track record. The next step is buying the first five dividend payers. When the first monthly payment comes in, it'll show up on the dashboard and go into crypto.

Why make it public

Two reasons. First, accountability. It's a lot harder to quietly break your own rules when anyone can read your trade log.

Second, I'm making YouTube Shorts and posting on X as @divtocrypto, and the goal is for that content to help fund the account's growth over time. A real log with real losses is more useful to people than another highlight reel, and it's the only version of this I'd want to put my name on.

What you can take from this

You don't need to copy my picks to borrow the structure:

  • Separate your engine from your bets. Decide which part of your money is supposed to be steady and which part is allowed to swing.
  • Write your rules before you trade. A rule you set while calm is easier to follow than one you invent mid-trade.
  • Keep a log with a "why" column. It will teach you more than memory ever will.
  • Be honest about where you are. A plan isn't results. Track the gap between the two.

I'll post weekly recaps here with the real numbers. If it works, you'll see it. If it doesn't, you'll see that too.
