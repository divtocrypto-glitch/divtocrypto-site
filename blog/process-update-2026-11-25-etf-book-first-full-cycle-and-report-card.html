What we tried: letting the ETF book run its full cycle

Quick refresher: the book buys big, liquid ETFs at about 2:55 PM Central after a sharp short-term drop. The rsi2 rule sells at the first close above the 5-day average (or after 10 trading days). The ibs rule sells when the price closes near the top of the day's range. Neither uses a stop, because neither was tested with one. The full rules are in How We Use Grok AI to Trade Stocks.

What worked: exits on bounce days

The useful lesson isn't the win rate (1 of 5). It's when the losers closed.

On Nov 20 the market slid hard. Grok Trader's end-of-day report marked the four losers at the official close:

Why? The rsi2 exit waits for the first daily close above the 5-day average, which usually means a rebound day. On Monday, Nov 23, SPY was trading about 4.7% above Friday's close before the open, and three positions hit their exit signals that afternoon. XLF followed on Nov 24.

That's the trade-off of a no-stop rule: you give up the safety of a stop, and in return you avoid selling at the low. This week it helped. In a market that keeps falling for weeks, the same rule would hold losers until the 10-day limit. One week proves nothing, so we judge each strategy only after at least 20 trades.

What didn't work: everything moved together

Four of the five closed trades were bought on the same afternoon (Nov 16). Owning SPY, XLB and XLV looked like three bets. In practice it was one bet on "the market bounces soon," and when the whole market dropped, all three lost at once.

We flagged this risk in Monday's scorecard post, and this week confirmed it.

Side effect: no cash left for breakouts

Each ETF position is up to a quarter of the account, so a full book uses nearly all the settled cash. That collided with the same-day breakout strategy:

  • On Nov 23, three morning breakout plans (NVDA, IREN and AXTI) couldn't be funded because settled cash was about one cent. NVDA and AXTI then triggered and hit their stops as paper trades, so the empty account saved two losses. That was luck, not a plan.
  • On Nov 24, an XLF breakout triggered on paper but was skipped because the ask was above the no-chase price. It then failed.

Daily plans and outcomes are on the Watchlist page, and every pass is explained on the skipped trades page.

What we're changing

  1. No more four-slot cap (Nov 20). The Owner approved opening extra ETF positions with spare cash, each up to a quarter of the account. Because the backtest only covered four slots, anything past four is tagged rsi2-beyond4 and scored separately. XLE is the first one.
  2. Cash sharing between strategies is the next decision. A full ETF book uses all settled cash, so slot sizing needs an Owner decision. Until then, unfunded breakout plans are logged as paper trades.
  3. Crypto trading stays paused. Every crypto rule we tested lost money once fees were included. A paper test this week, chasing fast pumps on 13 Solana-based pairs, produced one trade in 71 hours, far too few to judge, so it isn't going live.

FAQ

Why did the ETF book lose money when its backtest was profitable? Five trades is a tiny sample, and four were bought on the same day into a falling market. Backtests average many years, including losing stretches.

Why not just add a stop loss to the ETF trades? The rules were tested without stops. Adding one would mean running a different, untested strategy. This week, waiting for the rule-based exit cut the combined loss on four positions from -$16.20 at the Nov 20 close to -$7.60 at exit.

What does "rsi2-beyond4" mean? The tag for ETF positions opened beyond the first four. The original test used four slots, so extras are tracked separately.
