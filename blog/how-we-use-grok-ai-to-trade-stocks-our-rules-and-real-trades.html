How We Use Grok AI to Trade Stocks: Our Written Rules and Every Real Trade So Far

By DivToCrypto · Nov 22, 2026 · AI Trading Rules & Process · 8 min read

What does the AI actually do? The honest answer is less exciting than "the AI picks stocks." At DivToCrypto, Grok AI bots follow written rules that were tested before they went live. The bots scan, check, place and log trades. They aren't allowed to improvise.

This post lays out those rules in full, along with every real stock trade they've produced, straight from our trade log.

Educational, not financial advice. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. Trading involves risk, including the loss of money you put in.

The team: who does what

DivToCrypto runs on a small team of Grok AI bots. The Owner approves every rule change in writing, and a bot can't change a rule on its own.

  • Stock Finder scans for setups before and during the trading day and writes a plan for each: trigger, no-chase price, stop and target.
  • Chart Bot grades each setup (agree, caution or disagree) as a second opinion. Its grade never blocks a trade.
  • Grok Trader checks every signal against the rules and places the trades that pass.
  • The Watcher compares each fill with the quoted price and scores each strategy separately.

The background on how the team was built is in How I'm Using AI Bots (Grok Bot) to Run DivToCrypto.

The seven guardrails

On November 14, 2026, the Owner approved seven guardrails that apply to every bot:

  1. Fresh broker data only. Every decision uses quotes, prices and balances pulled live in that run. No remembered or estimated numbers.
  2. No overrides. Tested rules run exactly as written. News or a "high-confidence" read never changes a signal.
  3. Never size from confidence. Position size comes only from the written rules.
  4. Price every trade net of costs. If the edge doesn't clear the spread and fees, skip it.
  5. Exits are set at entry. They don't change mid-trade without the Owner's OK.
  6. Log every decision, including runs where nothing trades.
  7. Reconcile first. At the start of each run, broker cash and positions must match our records. If they don't, no new orders go in.

Guardrails 2 and 3 matter most: the biggest risk with an AI trader is a confident model talking itself out of a good rule.

Strategy 1: RSI(2) ETF dip buy (tag rsi2)

This is a short-term "buy the dip" rule, based on the 2-day RSI approach popularized by trader Larry Connors.

  • What it trades: 16 large ETFs only: SPY, QQQ, IWM, DIA, XLK, XLF, XLE, XLV, XLI, XLY, XLP, XLU, XLB, XLRE, XLC and SMH. No single stocks, and no inverse or leveraged funds.
  • Entry: at about 2:55 PM CT, just before the close, buy when the 2-day RSI is below 10 and the price is above its 200-day moving average. In plain terms: a sharp two-day pullback inside a longer uptrend.
  • Exit: sell at the first daily close above the 5-day moving average, or after 10 trading days, whichever comes first.
  • Stop: none. That's deliberate. The rule was tested without a stop, and dip-buy rules often need room to dip a little further before they bounce. Adding a stop the test never used would mean running an untested strategy.

Strategy 2: IBS ETF dip buy (tag ibs)

Internal bar strength (IBS) measures where the price sits within the day's range:

IBS = (price − day's low) ÷ (day's high − day's low)

An IBS near 0 means the price is near the day's low. Near 1 means it's near the high.

  • Universe: the same 16 ETFs.
  • Check time: about 2:55 PM CT. Exits are checked first.
  • Exit: sell when IBS is above 0.8, or the price is above the prior day's high.
  • Entry: buy an ETF we don't already hold when IBS is below 0.2. There's no 200-day filter and no stop.

In our team's backtest from 2005 to November 2026, with a 0.07% cost per side, the combined RSI(2) and IBS book returned about 11% a year and was profitable in 19 of 22 years. That also means it had losing years. Backtests aren't promises.

Strategy 3: Opening-range breakouts (tag orb)

This is the only same-day stock strategy still active. An opening-range breakout buys a stock that pushes above its early-morning high. Our version is heavily filtered:

  • Stocks in play only: gap-ups, unusual volume or top movers, priced at $5 or more, with a bid-ask spread of 0.3% or less, no earnings within three trading days, and no halts or share-dilution traps.
  • Relative volume: volume in the first 15 minutes (8:30–8:45 AM CT) must be at least 2x the average for that window over the prior 14 sessions.
  • Green first candle: the first 5-minute bar must close above its open.
  • Market filter: SPY must be trading above its intraday VWAP.
  • Reward-to-risk: the target must be at least 1.5x the distance to the stop, after spread, fees and a 0.05% slippage buffer.
  • Timing: no new entries after 11 AM CT. Exit by about 2:30 PM CT.
  • Failed breakouts: if a breakout closes the day back under its trigger, it's sold at the next open.

The filters exist because the unfiltered version lost money in testing. Over 46 days, plain ORB averaged -0.16R per trade (R is the amount risked). Adding the volume and green-candle filters with resting buy-stop entries brought that to +0.06R, a small edge that still has to prove itself live.

Stops and position sizing

Breakout trades always have a stop. When we can buy whole shares, Grok Trader places a resting buy-stop at the trigger (with a limit at the no-chase price), then a stop and target order as soon as it fills. Robinhood only allows stop orders on whole shares, so fractional positions use a "watched" stop that Grok Trader checks and executes. Once a trade is up by the amount risked (1R), the watched stop moves to break-even.

ETF dip buys have no stop, as tested. Their risk control is size and time: each new position is up to one-quarter of the account value, there's only one position per ETF, and RSI(2) trades are closed after 10 trading days at most. Until November 20, the book was capped at four positions, as in the backtest. The Owner then removed the cap. Positions beyond the fourth are tagged rsi2-beyond4 so they can be scored separately, because that part is untested.

A note for readers: many traders use a "risk 1% of the account per trade" rule. We don't, for now. Our sizing comes from the tested rules, and the account is small. If you're trading without a tested system, a fixed small risk per trade and a real stop are the simplest protection you can have.

How you can use Grok the same way

You don't need a bot team to use AI like this. The key is asking for rules and numbers, not predictions. Three prompts from our video series:

  • "Scan monthly dividend stocks yielding 8%+ and flag any dividend cuts."
  • "Give me an RSI(2) pullback setup on SPY with entry, exit and stop."
  • "Scan for stocks gapping up with 2x relative volume and give me an opening-range breakout plan with a stop."

Then check the answer against live data, because AI can be wrong about prices. You make the final call.

FAQ

Does Grok decide what to buy? No. Grok bots apply written rules to live data. They can't change the rules, size up because they feel confident, or override a signal because of news.

Why do the ETF trades have no stop-loss? Because the rules were tested without one. Their risk is limited by position size, a list of large diversified ETFs, and a 10-day time limit on RSI(2) trades.

How much money does the bot trade? It's a small account, and each ETF position has been about $74 to $102. Exact balances are private.

Is the strategy profitable? Too early to say. Two closed stock trades isn't a sample. Each strategy gets reviewed after 20 trades.
