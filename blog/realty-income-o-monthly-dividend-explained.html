Realty Income (O): The Monthly Dividend Company, Explained

By DivToCrypto · Nov 13, 2026 · 4 min read

Realty Income (ticker O) is probably the best-known monthly dividend stock in the US. It even trademarked the nickname "The Monthly Dividend Company." It sits at the top of our Top 30 monthly dividend stocks list and in the Core 10 of our 20 monthly dividend stocks plan. This post covers what the company actually does, what it pays, when it pays, what can go wrong, and where it fits in the DivToCrypto plan.

Educational only, not financial advice. We don't own O yet, and nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

What Realty Income is

Realty Income is a real estate investment trust, or REIT. A REIT owns property and has to pay out most of its taxable income to shareholders, which is why REITs tend to have higher yields than ordinary stocks.

Realty Income specializes in net-lease property. In a net lease, the tenant usually covers property taxes, insurance and maintenance, and the landlord mostly just collects rent. Leases tend to be long, so the rent checks are fairly predictable.

According to its Q3 2026 results, as of September 30, 2026 Realty Income:

  • owned or held interests in 15,536 properties
  • leased them to 1,842 clients across 96 industries
  • had 98.8% occupancy, with 182 properties available for lease or sale
  • had a weighted average remaining lease term of about 8.5 years

Its 10-Q filing breaks down annualized base rent as about 77.0% retail, 17.0% industrial, 3.1% gaming, 1.0% data centers and 1.9% other. The Q3 supplemental shows about 79.6% of rent comes from the United States, 15.1% from the United Kingdom and 5.3% from continental Europe.

The dividend: amount, yield and schedule

On November 10, 2026, Realty Income declared a raise in its monthly dividend to $0.2740 per share, up from $0.2715. That works out to $3.288 per share per year. The company says it's the 137th dividend increase since it listed on the NYSE in 1994. Seeking Alpha put the raise at 0.9% and the forward yield at about 5.52%.

It pays every month. Here are the most recent dates, from StockAnalysis:

To get the December 15 payment, you'd need to own the shares before the November 30 ex-dividend date. Notice how small the raises are: a fraction of a cent every few months. Realty Income is about slow, steady growth, not big jumps.

Is the dividend covered?

You'll sometimes see scary payout ratios quoted for Realty Income, like over 200%. That number compares the dividend to GAAP net income, which includes a large non-cash charge for depreciation. REITs are usually judged on AFFO (adjusted funds from operations), which is closer to the cash the business actually generates.

Realty Income reported Q3 2026 AFFO of $1.12 per share, up 3.7% from a year earlier, and raised its full-year 2026 AFFO guidance to $4.45 to $4.46 per share (source). Comparing the new $3.288 annual dividend to the $4.455 midpoint, the dividend is about 74% of expected AFFO. That's our own math on the company's numbers, and it leaves some cushion.

The risks

A steady history doesn't mean no risk. The main ones we watch:

  • Interest rates. REITs borrow a lot, and when Treasury yields rise, a 5.5% dividend looks less special. MarketBeat points out that higher Treasury yields can weigh on REIT dividends' appeal. The share price can fall even when the dividend keeps rising.
  • Slow growth. Raises under 1% at a time won't make anyone rich quickly. This is a "get paid while you wait" holding.
  • Tenant trouble. Most rent comes from retail. If big tenants struggle, vacancies and rent cuts follow, even with 98.8% occupancy today.
  • Share issuance. Realty Income grows by buying property, often funded by selling new shares. If those deals don't earn enough, per-share growth stalls.
  • Currency. About a fifth of rent comes from the UK and Europe, so exchange rates matter.
