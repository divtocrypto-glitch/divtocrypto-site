Monthly Dividend Stocks: A Complete Beginner's Guide (With Data From Our Top 30 List)

By DivToCrypto · Nov 22, 2026 · Monthly Dividend Stocks · 7 min read

Most companies that pay dividends pay them four times a year. A smaller group pays every month. Those monthly dividend stocks are the foundation of DivToCrypto: we buy monthly payers and reinvest the payouts into trading stocks and crypto.

This guide explains how monthly dividend stocks work, using real numbers from our Top 30 monthly dividend stocks list, last refreshed on November 21, 2026. It's written for beginners, but the data should be useful even if you've been investing for years.

What is a monthly dividend stock?

A dividend is cash a company or fund pays to its shareholders, usually out of profits or income. A monthly dividend stock pays that cash twelve times a year instead of four.

Monthly payers aren't a special kind of security. They're a payment schedule. Some business types lean toward monthly payments because their income comes in steadily, like rent checks or loan interest. That's why most monthly payers are real estate investment trusts (REITs), business development companies (BDCs) or income funds.

How dividend yield works

Dividend yield is the yearly payout as a percentage of the price. Our Top 30 list calculates it like this:

Yield = latest monthly payout × 12 ÷ current price

Take Realty Income (O). In November 2026 it declared a monthly dividend of $0.2740 per share (press release). That's $3.288 a year. At the $58.03 price in our list, the yield is about 5.6%.

To turn yield into dollars, multiply. $1,000 invested at a 5.62% yield pays about $56.20 a year, or roughly $4.68 a month. Yield changes every day because the price changes, even when the payout doesn't.

Why monthly instead of quarterly?

There are three common reasons people like monthly payers.

  1. Cash flow that matches bills. Rent, utilities and car payments are monthly. Income that arrives on the same rhythm is easier to budget.
  2. Faster reinvestment. Each payout can go back to work sooner. In our case, it funds the trading side every month instead of every quarter.
  3. Earlier warning signs. A cut shows up within weeks instead of months.

There's one benefit that gets oversold: compounding. If a 6% yield is reinvested monthly for 10 years, with no change in price, $1 grows to about $1.819. Reinvested quarterly, it grows to about $1.814. That's our own math, and the difference is under half a percent over a decade. Pick monthly payers for the cash flow, not for magic compounding.

Five checks before you trust a monthly dividend

1. Is the payout covered? Each business type has its own income measure. For REITs, compare the dividend with adjusted funds from operations (AFFO). Realty Income's $3.288 annual dividend is about 74% of the midpoint of its 2026 AFFO guidance, by our math on company figures. For BDCs, compare it with net investment income (NII). Main Street Capital reported Q3 2026 NII of $0.89 per share against $0.795 of regular monthly dividends (source).

2. What's the cut history? A company that cut in 2020 isn't automatically bad, but repeated cuts are a pattern. Our list counts cuts and suspensions over 10 years.

3. Is book value shrinking? For mortgage REITs and BDCs, falling book value or net asset value often comes before a cut. AGNC's tangible net book value fell 16.9% in one quarter, to $7.13 per share at September 30, 2026 (AGNC Q3 results).

4. Is the payout fixed or variable? Covered-call ETFs and royalty trusts change their payout month to month. The yield you see today is a snapshot.

5. How big is it? Smaller companies can be fine, but they have less room for error. Our score gives up to 15 points for size.

FAQ

Are monthly dividend stocks better than quarterly ones? Not by themselves. The payment schedule doesn't make a business stronger. What matters is whether the payout is covered and sustainable.

How much do I need to invest to get $100 a month? At a 6% yield, $100 a month ($1,200 a year) takes about $20,000. At 5%, it takes $24,000. Higher yields need less money, but they usually come with more risk of a cut.

Are monthly dividends taxed differently? The schedule doesn't change the tax. The type of payer does. Many REIT and BDC dividends aren't "qualified" dividends, so in a taxable account they're often taxed at ordinary income rates. See IRS Publication 550 or a tax professional.

What's the safest monthly dividend stock? No dividend is guaranteed. In our list, the names with long records, no recent cuts and moderate yields score highest. O is ranked first, but it can still fall in price or cut someday.
