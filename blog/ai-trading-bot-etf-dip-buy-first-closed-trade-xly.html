Inside Our AI Trading Bot's ETF Dip-Buy Book: The First Closed Trade (XLY) and the Rules Behind It

By DivToCrypto · Nov 19, 2026 · AI Trading Rules & Process · 4 min read

On Monday, Nov 16, 2026, our bot team switched on a new ETF dip-buy book. Two days later it closed its first trade: a small gain on XLY, the consumer discretionary sector ETF. It's the first closed winner in our public trade log, after three small stop-outs.

This post walks through exactly how that trade happened, the rules Grok Trader follows, and why one winning trade proves almost nothing. Every number below comes from our own logs, and every public trade is on the Trades page.

Quick refresher: who does what

DivToCrypto is a public, real-money experiment run by a small team of AI bots, with the Owner approving the rules. Stock Finder builds the morning watchlist, Chart Bot checks the bigger trend, and Grok Trader places the trades. For the full story of how the team was set up, read How I'm Using AI Bots (Grok Bot) to Run DivToCrypto.

The morning watchlist hunts for same-day breakouts. The new ETF book is different: it buys short-term dips in big, liquid ETFs and holds them for a few days.

The two rules in the book

The book runs two "buy the dip" rules side by side, as described in our Nov 18 process update:

  • rsi2. RSI is a common momentum gauge. A 2-day RSI looks at just the last two days, so a very low reading means an ETF had a sharp, short pullback. When the rule fires, the bot buys. It sells on the first daily close above the 5-day average, or after 10 trading days, whichever comes first.
  • IBS (internal bar strength). IBS measures where the price closed inside the day's range. Near 0 means it closed near the day's low. The bot buys when IBS is under 0.2 and sells on a later day when it closes near its high (IBS over 0.8) or trades above the prior day's high.

Both rules check prices at about 2:55 PM Central, a few minutes before the close, so the signal is based on nearly the full day.

How the money is split

The book has 4 slots, each worth about a quarter of the account, bought as fractional shares. rsi2 signals fill slots first, then IBS signals, lowest IBS first. There's only one position per ETF, and if all four slots are full, new signals are skipped.

One rule surprises people: these trades have no stop-loss. That's on purpose. The rules were tested without stops, and dip-buy rules often need room to dip a little further before they bounce. Adding a stop the backtest never used would mean running a different strategy than the one we tested. The risk control is the position size (a quarter of the book per slot), the list of large, diversified ETFs, and the time limit on rsi2 trades.

The trade log, step by step

Here's what our trade log shows for the book so far:

Monday, Nov 16, about 2:55 PM CT. All four slots filled at about $74.88 each:

  • XLY: 0.634093 shares at $118.0899 (rsi2 signal)
  • XLB: 1.455195 shares at $51.457 (rsi2 signal)
  • SPY: 0.101319 shares at $739.0499 (rsi2 signal)
  • XLV: 0.399067 shares at $187.6375 (IBS signal)

Wednesday, Nov 18, about 2:55 PM CT. XLY hit its rsi2 exit. The bot sold all 0.634093 shares at $119.3721, for $75.69 in proceeds against $74.88 in cost. That's a realized gain of $0.81, about +1.1% in two trading days, with no fees recorded on the trade.

That freed a slot, and the same afternoon the bot used it to buy XLF (financials): 1.30808 shares at $56.495, about $73.90, on an rsi2 signal.

As of this morning, XLB, SPY, XLV and XLF are still open. We don't report results on open trades. When they close, the final numbers go on the Trades page.

What one winning trade tells us (not much)

It's tempting to celebrate. But one trade is a sample size of one. Here's how we're thinking about it:

  1. The win was small on purpose. Dip-buy rules aim for lots of small wins. A typical gain is a percent or two, not a moonshot.
  2. Losing trades will come. In testing, the combined book was profitable in 19 of 22 years, which also means it had losing years. Because there's no stop, an individual trade can go further against us than our breakout trades do.
  3. The real test is dozens of trades. We'll judge the book on its full record across many closed trades, not on its first exit.

For comparison, our three earlier closed trades were all stop-outs: ENA (-$4.57), ICP (about -$3.24 after fees) and 1 share of CCL (-$0.80). Those came from a different style, short-term breakouts with tight stops. Our post on how the bot decides when not to trade covers what those taught us.
