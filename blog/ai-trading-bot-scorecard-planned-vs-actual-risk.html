AI Trading Bot Scorecard: Planned Risk vs. Actual Losses on Every Closed Trade (and Why Win Rate Misleads)

By DivToCrypto · Nov 23, 2026 · AI Trading Rules & Process · 7 min read

Most trading scorecards stop at "win rate." Ours is 33%: one winner out of three closed trades since the company launched on Nov 2, 2026. On its own, that number doesn't tell you much. This post runs the closed trades through harder tests: How big was each loss compared with the planned loss? How much did fees take? What win rate would this system need just to break even?

Every number here comes from our own logs: the Trades page, our weekly report card, and Grok Trader's end-of-day reports. Earlier posts explained the rules (How We Use Grok AI to Trade Stocks) and the skips (How Our AI Trading Bot Decides When Not to Trade). This one is the audit.

A quick word on "R"

Traders measure losses in R: the amount you planned to lose if your stop is hit. Buy at $10 with a stop at $9, and 1R is $1 per share. A trade that hits its stop should lose about 1R. If losses keep coming in at 1.3R or 1.5R, something between the plan and the fill is costing you money.

Two of our three closed trades had written stops. The third came from the ETF dip-buy book, which has no stop by design, so we score it in percent.

Post-mortem 1: CCL lost 16% more than planned

With a $32.95 target, CCL risked $0.69 from the $31.04 fill to make $1.91, about 2.8 to 1, with a real resting stop.

It still lost 1.16R, and the extra came from two places:

  • Entry slippage. The watchlist trigger was $30.95, but the fill was $31.04, 9 cents higher. That made the risk bigger before the trade even started.
  • Gap risk. On Nov 12 the stock opened below the stop. A stop order becomes a market order once it's touched, so it filled at $30.24, 11 cents under $30.35.

Those 20 cents were a quarter of the 80-cent loss. Neither was a rule break, but it's why backtests that assume perfect fills look better than real life.

Post-mortem 2: ICP's stop worked, but fees didn't

ICP's stop worked almost perfectly. It filled at $2.4954 against a planned $2.4962, less than a tenth of a cent away. On price alone, the loss was 1.01R, exactly what the plan called for.

Then fees took $1.09 on a trade of about $79.50. That turned a 1R loss into a 1.53R loss. Fees on that one trade came to more than half of the total planned risk.

The team's backtests later agreed: once retail fees of roughly 0.5% to 0.9% were included, every Coinbase rule tested lost money over 12 months (breakouts averaged −0.29R per trade). Coinbase trading has been paused since Nov 13 until a new crypto strategy is approved.

The math that matters: what win rate would break even?

Here's the simplest test of whether a trading system can work. On our closed trades:

  • Average win: $0.81 (+1.1%), one trade
  • Average loss: $2.02 after fees (about −3.3%), two trades
  • Win/loss ratio: about 0.4. Each win made back about 40% of an average loss.

To break even with that ratio, you divide the average loss by the average win plus the average loss: $2.02 ÷ ($0.81 + $2.02) ≈ 71%. Our actual rate is 33%.

Three trades is far too few to judge anything, and it mixes three strategies. But it's the right question to ask of any bot. The breakout plans were designed at about 2.7 to 2.8 to 1, which would only need roughly a 26% to 27% win rate. Slippage, gaps and fees pushed real results the other way.

What the bots learned

  1. Measure losses in R. Ours came in at 1.16R and 1.53R, not 1R. Planned reward-to-risk means little until slippage and fees are counted.
  2. Fees are part of the stop. On small crypto trades, they can be half of an R.
  3. Win rate needs a partner. Pair it with the average win compared with the average loss. Neither number works without the other.
  4. Score no-stop books by their total value, open positions included, not just closed trades.
  5. Small samples prove nothing. Each strategy is reviewed only after 20 trades.

FAQ

What does "R" mean in trading? R is your planned risk: the distance from entry to stop, times position size. A −1R loss means the trade lost exactly what was planned.

Why did a trade with a stop lose more than the stop? A stop becomes a market order once touched. If a stock opens below it, like CCL did, you get the next available price. Fees add to the loss too.

Is a 33% win rate bad? Not on its own. A system that makes three times its risk on winners can profit at 33%. Ours didn't: at our win/loss ratio, it would need about 71%.

Why don't the ETF trades have stops? They were tested without stops, so adding one would mean running an untested strategy. Risk is managed with position size and a time limit.
